## The game

**The look: futuristic neon, synthwave sundown into night.** A giant striped synthwave sun sinks on the horizon in a magenta-to-violet sky; as it goes down, stars and a big ringed planet come out, and a distant megacity skyline lights up window by window. The ground is dark and glossy with a glowing grid. The road is wet-look and reflective, with light-strip edges, glowing lane lines and chevrons that pulse on the corners. Cars leave Tron-style light trails (these replace v1's drift smoke), with underglow, emissive livery strips, real headlight beams once night falls, and tail-light streaks. Boost pads are glowing arrows, loops are rings of light, wall rides are half-pipe tubes, and holographic billboards line the run. Crash props are neon crates and energy cubes that shatter into glowing shards, and the collectibles are energy cores. Bloom is the whole look, so treat it as a budgeted system, not a slider. Boost kicks the FOV, adds chromatic aberration and speed lines. Time of day runs from sundown into full night and is a setting, so headlights matter. The reference class is the best three.js / react-three-fiber showcase work, the award-site demos people share; if a screenshot wouldn't make someone stop scrolling, keep going. Neon is easy to do badly: avoid the generic defaults (flat purple gradients everywhere, glow on everything so nothing reads, unlit black voids, pure-primary colours). Glow has to guide the eye to the road and the car, and the scene still needs depth: near, mid and far planes, atmosphere and haze, and the sun's direction obvious.

**Tracks are data. This is the foundation, so get it right first.** Every track is one file in a `tracks/` folder, in a documented format (`tracks/README.md`) that holds everything the track needs: the road's centreline with per-point width and bank angle, special surface pieces (loops, wall rides), boost pads, ramps, crash props, collectible spots, the start and spawn, and the environment (terrain recipe, sky and time-of-day defaults, palette accents). The game builds the whole world from that file. Lap validity checkpoints, the Ai racing line, spawn grids, the minimap and the ghost all derive from it automatically. The test of the foundation: I should be able to ask a future Claude session for a new track, and it writes one file and it plays, with nothing else in the codebase touched.

**The road is its own 3D ribbon, not paint on the terrain.** A spline with a bank (roll) value at every point, built as its own mesh and collider, with the terrain blended and flattened up to its edges. That is what makes adjustable banking, loops, wall rides and bridges possible; v1's heightfield-only world can't do overhangs.

**Two tracks at launch:**
- **The fun track:** an open neon world you can leave the road in. A flowing circuit with jumps, a loop, a wall ride, boost pads, crash-prop clusters and the energy-core hunt scattered off-road. Include a big-air hill built to this exact spec (v1 took three rebuilds to land on it): a big natural hill you can climb from any side, a dip, then a small mountain to launch off. Don't add decorative gates, arches or gantries; every version we tried read as weird.
- **The Hyperdrome:** an enclosed neon stadium oval for testing top speed. The banked curves' angle is adjustable live with a slider (from flat to steep), and a speed trap on the straight shows and records top speed.

**The road editor.** Josh draws tracks. Top-down view of a track's world: draw the road freehand like a pencil, and the game smooths it, enforces a minimum corner radius, banks corners automatically from their curvature (with a per-section bank override), flattens the terrain under it and closes the loop. Then place pieces: boost pads, ramps, loops, wall rides, crash props, energy cores and the start line. Undo works. A "Test drive" button drops you straight onto it. Tracks save locally and export/import as the same track file the built-in tracks use, so Josh can swap tracks with friends and drop them into `tracks/`. Also add "drive to draw": a mode where the car lays road behind it as you drive, which then cleans up into a track. Terrain sculpting (raising your own hills) is a stretch goal only if everything else is done and checked. The editor's top-down view doubles as the world map, so v1's separate map generator isn't needed.

**Cars and feel.** Wheeled cars on the proven v1 raycast-suspension physics, retuned for this world. Several futuristic procedural bodies in a garage on the title screen, with paint, underglow and light-trail colours. Loops and wall rides use magnetic grip: on those surfaces, enough speed keeps the car stuck to them; too slow and it falls off. The feel standard (each of these cost v1 a rework round):
- A quick handbrake tap with hands off self-straightens; a held drift stays alive and is catchable.
- Braking mid-corner never spins the car (brake balance plus a stability setting).
- Steering is speed-sensitive, keyboard steering is smoothed so it doesn't twitch, and sensitivity is a setting.
- Landings are forgiving (a short two-wheel recovery window), but landing on the roof is a wipeout.
- Air control is generous but calm, and the engine free-revs in the air rather than falling silent.
- Springs and damping on everything that moves: camera, car body, HUD. Physics at a fixed 60Hz with interpolated rendering, so it stays smooth on Josh's 165Hz screen. Nothing snaps except an explicit reset.
- Controls: keyboard and Xbox controller via the Gamepad API, analog steering and triggers, hot-swap with no config (whichever was touched last wins). Camera cycle (chase, close, bonnet), R resets to the road, Shift+R restarts at the start line, Esc opens the menu.

**Play.** Carry over everything from v1 that worked: trick scoring (air, spins, flips, barrel rolls, combos; scored only at landing so a crash voids the combo, spins counted by integrating angular rate, no participation points), crash props that burst for points and re-scatter each round, smashable roadside pieces (solid when hit slow, smash when hit fast, consistently), the energy-core hunt with a clock, the ghost of your best lap per track, persisted best laps and high scores, and lap validity (ordered sector checkpoints plus a dirty-lap rule so cutting the course or long off-road stretches can't set a record). Modes:
- **Free roam** with tricks, props and the hunt.
- **Time trial** against your ghost.
- **Race** against Ai racers: choose 0 to 5 of them, with a difficulty slider and gentle catch-up. They drive the derived racing line on any track, drawn ones included, use boost pads, and are solid to collide with.
- **Stunt score attack:** a timed run for the biggest trick score.
- **Multiplayer** (LAN, below): race, free roam and tag. In tag, one player is "it" and glows; bumping someone passes it on; least total time as "it" wins.

**Multiplayer: reuse the proven v1 setup.** `bun run mp` on the host starts the relay plus vite `--host` and prints join links; everyone else just opens a link (`?mp=1&name=...&color=...`), nothing installed. Each client simulates only its own car; remote cars are interpolated kinematic rapier bodies, so collisions genuinely shove you; synced race starts from a deterministic grid; crash-prop rounds are shared. New for v2: the host's track, drawn tracks included, streams to everyone who joins. Ai racers stay solo-only.

**Music.** v1 had none. Build a procedural synthwave soundtrack in WebAudio: drums, bass, arpeggios and pads, generated in code so it is never quite the same twice, with intensity that rises with speed, boost and race state, and a different mood per track. Music and effects have separate volume sliders, and the mix ducks sensibly under crashes. The engine sound fits the neon world while still tracking rpm and speed.

**Settings and Josh's door.** An in-game settings menu, reachable from the title screen and the pause menu, fully navigable with the Xbox controller: car and colours, handling (grip, steering, stability, power, brakes), camera, audio volumes, time of day, Ai racer count and difficulty, the Hyperdrome bank angle, and graphics quality (low, medium, high, auto; neon bloom is heavy on a laptop). Settings persist locally. `src/core/config.ts` stays as the one well-commented kid-facing knob file holding the defaults; Josh edits a value, saves, and sees it change. Keep the game-event feed (tricks, laps, crashes, pickups, race results) clean and documented, because an Ai radio commentator will plug into it later; don't build the commentator.

**Josh's setup and the Windows path.** Josh plays on a Windows gaming laptop (RTX 4060, 165Hz screen) with a Bluetooth Xbox controller. Ship the v1-style launchers, adapted: `Sundown Run Two.bat` (play; finds Bun or Node, installs on first run), `Sundown Run Two Multiplayer.bat` (installs Bun if missing, adds the inbound firewall rule for 5201-5202 via a one-time admin prompt, and calls binaries directly because PowerShell's execution policy blocks the `.ps1` shims), and `Sundown Run Two Update.bat` (fetch plus hard reset plus clean, by design). Pin `.bat` files to CRLF in `.gitattributes`. The README covers Windows setup in plain words, including the fix for Windows handing the browser the integrated GPU (Settings, System, Display, Graphics, High performance) and how to check which GPU is in use. At the end, generate a `Learn To Code.html` workshop for Josh by name (`bun run learn`), with runnable in-page playgrounds and missions anchored to real `file:line` locations in this codebase, the way v1's was. Write the project's `CLAUDE.md` so a future session can pick it up.

- Multiplayer works across two browser clients: join, see each other, collide, race, tag, and a drawn track reaches the joiner.

## How to run the build

Run this as an orchestrated team build, not a solo marathon. You are the boss and art director: you write the standard, design the system, review the work and rule on disputes. Route the implementation to parallel worker sub-agents (Agent tool, `model: "opus"`), spawned fresh rather than forked so each starts from its own brief. Split the work along whatever seams you judge natural (track format and road ribbon, world and environment, vehicle and feel, look and post, audio and music, editor, game modes and Ai racers, multiplayer, UI and settings are the obvious candidates). Keep workers long-lived so their context compounds, and keep your own hands for the constitution, the hardest glue and final judgement.

Before the first line of game code, write the constitution: one short document in the repo covering the art direction (palette, light, glow rules, the reference class), the performance budget (the 60fps line, bloom and draw-call budgets, instancing, no per-frame allocation, no React state per frame), and the feel standard above. Then freeze the shared contracts before workers start: the track file schema, the road ribbon API, telemetry, the game-event feed, the store, config and settings, and the measurement handles the perf checks read. Give each worker strict file ownership; cross-boundary edits go through you. If the direction genuinely changes mid-build, update the constitution deliberately.

Every worker task ends with an independent check by a fresh-context sub-agent that didn't write the code. Checkers re-execute rather than re-read: they drive the running game, take screenshots, measure frame times and read the console, and judge against the constitution, never against the worker's report. Failures go back to the worker with the specific defect, and the loop repeats until true. No rank is exempt, and if a checker's verdict looks wrong, settle it against the constitution rather than overruling it quietly. Named checks: a visual judge on real screenshots of both tracks at sundown and at night; a performance check over the scripted demo drive per track; a feel check (hands-off handbrake tap, braking mid-corner, landings, loops and wall rides); the track-as-one-file and editor round-trip proofs above; a two-client multiplayer check; and a clean-clone check near the end. Add whatever else the build needs. Checkers verify a built preview on its own port, not the dev server workers are editing, and confirm which commit they're looking at. Keep one WebGL tab per checker; several at once crashed Chrome last time and took the DevTools MCP down with it.

You and your checkers have the Chrome DevTools MCP (open tabs in the background, set the viewport to 1920x1080). Build whatever dev-only affordances make checking cheap and repeatable: `?demo=1` autopilot drives with frame timing into `window.__perf`, fixed camera bookmarks, a time-of-day override, a track loader, a screenshot hotkey, inspection handles on `window`. "Looks good" can't be asserted; point to a screenshot from this session that shows it.

Known traps from v1, so nobody relearns them: rapier heightfields are infinitely thin and fast falls tunnel through (CCD won't arm), so keep a catch floor and a below-ground auto-reset; probe the heightfield's index order before freezing the terrain API; `mergeGeometries` returns `null` silently on mixed indexed and non-indexed input; coplanar faces z-fight, so separate them geometrically, never with `polygonOffset`; fogging far scenery to a solid colour makes a slab, so dissolve it by vertex alpha; never drive a kinematic body through the declarative `<RigidBody>`, create it imperatively; put a NaN firewall on every value entering rapier; a gamepad press isn't a browser gesture, so a pad-only player never unlocks WebAudio unless you handle it; capture the arrow keys on menus so they don't leak into throttle; Vite HMR orphans Web Workers and AudioContexts unless `import.meta.hot.dispose()` shuts them down; and every reachable part of every track, drawn ones included, must physically contain the car.

Josh and I will be playing as you build, and last time that loop drove more quality than the spec did. Keep the dev server running on 5201 for us from the first runnable build, tell me when there's something worth playing, and route our reports to the owning worker.

Keep a checklist in `TASKS.md`. Tick each item when it's done, and add anything new you find. Runs this long get summarised, and the file survives that. Never stop or pause because your context is filling up; keep working and let auto-compact run, and your checklist and commits carry the state across it.

Before reporting progress on anything, audit each claim against a tool result from this session. If something fails or underperforms, say so with the output. Treat your own earlier status notes, and every worker's report, as a reconstruction, not a readout. Some things can't be checked from the harness: the physical Xbox controller, the `.bat` files on Windows, the firewall rule, multiplayer across two real machines, and Josh's laptop's frame rate. Keep those paths simple and standards-based and flag each for a human test.

When you have enough to act, act. The actions here are reversible and follow from this request, so proceed end to end without asking. On a genuinely ambiguous fork, state your assumption and keep going. Stop and ask only when you can't continue without me, or before anything destructive or outside `~/Dev/SundownRun2` other than the reserved-ports rows. Never modify `~/Dev/SundownRun`; read from it only.
